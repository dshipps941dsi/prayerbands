Someone you love is a year sober. Or thirty days. Or they had a week that nearly undid them and they showed up anyway. You want to mark it, and you are not sure what is appropriate.

Here is what people in recovery say actually lands, what to avoid, and how to give a gift that keeps showing up on the days between milestones.

## First, what the rooms already do well

Twelve-step programs mark time with chips and medallions, and they do it in the room, in front of people who know what it cost. Do not compete with that. A gift from you is not a replacement for the chip. It is the thing that goes with them on Tuesday morning when there is no meeting and nobody is clapping.

Two things to keep in mind:

- **Anonymity is not a formality.** It is the reason people can walk into the room at all. Nothing you give should announce, on its face, that the person is in recovery. No program names, no slogans on the outside, nothing a coworker could read across a table.
- **The program is theirs, not yours.** Give the gift one-to-one. Do not hand things out at a meeting, and do not tie a gift to a group that has its own traditions about outside contributions.

## Gifts that work

**1. A plain band with one line on it.** A verse, a word, "one day at a time," nothing that names a program. It goes on the wrist and it stays there. If it is a prayer band, the outside says only what you chose to print, and the inside carries more, which we will get to.

**2. A letter for the hard day, sealed.** Write it now, while things are good. Address the envelope "for the day you need it." They will know when.

**3. A coffee mug for the morning after a meeting.** Mornings are where a lot of recovery happens. Something in the hand that says you noticed.

**4. A journal with the first page already written.** One paragraph from you. Not advice. What you have watched them do.

**5. A meal, on the date, no speech.** Milestone dinners are better when you do not make a toast. Show up, eat, say "I'm proud of you" once, and let it be.

**6. Something for the sponsor.** If your person has a sponsor who has carried them, a quiet thank-you goes further than you would guess. A band, a card, a coffee.

**7. Your own commitment, written down.** "I will pray for you every morning this year." Then do it, and every so often tell them you did.

## What a prayer band adds

A plain band on the wrist reminds. A prayer band does that, and it carries something the person can reach on a bad day without telling anyone.

- **The outside is whatever you print.** A verse, a word, a date. Nothing that reads as recovery to a stranger.
- **The inside opens to a page.** When they tap the band with their phone, they see the verse, a note you wrote them, and the prayers of the people who have prayed over it. That note is the letter for the hard day, without the envelope.
- **You keep praying, and they can see it.** Tap Pray on their band's page and the count goes up. On a Tuesday morning, "seven people prayed over this band this week" is a real thing to look at.
- **It stays private.** The band has no battery and no location. It does not know who is wearing it, and nothing is written back to it. A prayer left on the public wall can be anonymous. Their own journal is private to them.
- **It can be passed on.** When they are ready, they can hand the band to someone else who is starting out, with their own note inside, and stay in its story as the one who gave it.

## A word about the milestone itself

If you give a band on the day, keep it small. Hand it over after, not during. Say what it is, say what is inside it, and let them decide when to tap it. Some people open the note that night. Some wait for the day they need it. Both are right.

## When it is not your place

If you are not sure the person wants their recovery acknowledged at all, do not acknowledge it. Give the band because you are praying for them. That is true either way, and it needs no explanation.
